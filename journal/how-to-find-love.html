How to Find Love

Not luck, not an algorithm. Most people who find a good relationship get there through a handful of ordinary things done consistently.

6 min readField notes from Fewer Better Dates

Ask a happy couple how they met, and the answer is almost never "an app got it exactly right." It's usually something duller and more useful: a shared friend group, a class, a job, a hobby, a wedding. Love tends to show up where two people were already spending time around the same things, not where an algorithm decided to match two photos.

That doesn't mean apps can't work. It means the mechanism that actually finds love is overlap, not luck, and you can build overlap on purpose.

1. Go where the repetition is

A single date with a stranger asks a lot of two people who've never spent time together. A running club, a pottery class or a regular pub quiz gives you the same person, in the same context, six or ten times, which is a much better way to actually get to know someone. Recurring activities beat one-off dates for building real attraction, because attraction usually needs more than one data point.

2. Let attraction be a slow burn, not a verdict

Most good relationships didn't start with fireworks. They started with "I liked talking to them" and grew from there.

Instant chemistry feels like the strongest signal, so it's tempting to use it as a filter. But a lot of people who ended up in long relationships describe the early days as pleasant rather than electric. If you write someone off after one quiet date, you may be discarding exactly the kind of connection that tends to last.

3. Say what you actually want, early

Vague signals waste everyone's time. If you want something serious, say so by the second or third conversation. This filters out people who don't want the same thing faster, and it draws in the people who do, because most people relax once they know they're not going to be misread.

4. Reduce the number of people you're evaluating at once

This is the part dating apps get backwards. A huge pool of options feels like more opportunity, but it actually makes people harder to evaluate fairly and easier to write off. Focusing on two or three real prospects at a time, instead of forty half-read profiles, tends to produce better decisions and less burnout.

5. Ask people who know you

A friend who's watched you date for years often has a clearer read on your patterns than you do. Ask them directly: who do you know who might actually be right for me, and why? It's an old method, and it still works better than most modern ones, because a person who knows you is already doing the filtering an app can't.
