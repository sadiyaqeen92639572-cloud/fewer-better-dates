Why Is Dating So Hard Right Now?

Most single people feel like dating has gotten harder, and there's a real reason for that beyond bad luck.

6 min readField notes from Fewer Better Dates

If you've felt tired of dating apps lately, you're not imagining it. Ask around and you'll hear some version of the same story: swipe for a while, get a handful of matches, message for a few days, then nothing. A lot of single people say dating feels worse now than it did a few years ago, even with more apps to choose from than ever.

More options were supposed to make this easier. For a lot of people it hasn't, and there are specific reasons dating apps don't work the way they're supposed to.

Dating apps are built to keep you on the app

A dating app's business is engagement: more swipes, more sessions, more time spent in the app. Two users who meet, fall for each other and delete the app are, from that business's point of view, a pair of lost users.

That doesn't mean the people who built these apps want you single. It means the incentive doesn't line up with yours. An algorithm built to keep you swiping and an algorithm built to help you meet someone and leave are two different products, and most dating apps are, by design, the first one.

Dating apps optimise for time spent in the app. They don't optimise for time spent with the right person.

The paradox of choice makes everyone pickier and less satisfied

Psychologists have a name for what happens when you're shown an endless stream of options: the paradox of choice. More choice doesn't make you more likely to pick well. It makes you less confident in whatever you pick, because there's always another profile one swipe away that might be better.

On a dating app, this shows up as a specific kind of dating app fatigue: nothing feels wrong enough to fully commit to, and nothing feels right enough to stop looking. You end up half-invested in several conversations instead of fully invested in one.

Everyone's playing a different game

A lot of dating-app frustration comes down to one thing: mismatched intentions. Some people want a life partner. Others want something casual. Both are fine on their own, but when the app doesn't sort for that upfront, you spend the first three messages finding out which one you're actually talking to.

One of the most common complaints about dating apps isn't that the people on them are bad. It's that nobody knows, before matching, whether the other person actually wants the same thing.

So what actually helps?

More matches isn't the fix. What seems to actually work is fewer, more carefully considered introductions instead of a big, loosely filtered pool. A few things make dating feel less exhausting:

  • A hard limit on how many people you're introduced to at once, so you can actually invest in them instead of half-watching a queue.
  • Clarity on intentions upfront, so you're not spending three weeks of messages to find out someone wants something different.
  • A nudge to meet in person early, because compatibility over text is a weak signal for compatibility in a room.
  • Feedback that actually changes your next introduction, instead of a static profile shown to everyone the same way.

This is roughly how a good matchmaker, or a setup from a friend, already works. An intentional dating approach just tries to rebuild that on purpose, at a scale bigger than your own social circle.
